## H0 -- data integrity  (E00)
- Finding: Joins are 1:1 and match the raw files. 4589 of 46266 hires (9.9%) are right-censored and excluded, leaving 41677 hires with an observed six-month outcome. 530 hires have a different ATS at start than at application; we use the application-month ATS throughout because that is when the essay was written.
- Spec: n/a -- data validation
- Sample: All raw files; hires_obs = hires with retained_6mo not null
- Verdict: supported -- starter.load() joins are correct; 7 centers never migrated (matches DATA-DICTIONARY.md and prompt.pdf, which say 7).

## H1 -- retention decline concentrated in new-ATS, post-AI hires  (E01/E02/E03)
- Finding: Six-month retention on the new ATS falls 2.1% more than on legacy after the AI era begins (DiD estimate -0.0207, 95% CI [-0.0376, -0.0037], n=41,677, controls and center/start-month FE). Reopen rates rise 0.25 points more (95% CI [0.07, 0.42]). By separation reason: voluntary: +0.0111 [+0.0013, +0.0210] (distinguishable from zero); performance: +0.0064 [-0.0035, +0.0164] (not distinguishable from zero); attendance: +0.0031 [-0.0044, +0.0106] (not distinguishable from zero). The extra separations are voluntary, not performance-driven terminations -- the honest reading is that new hires screened on an inflated essay are finding they cannot or do not want to do the job, and are leaving on their own, rather than being caught and fired for poor performance. That is consistent with a worse screen (people who interview and read well but were never going to stick), but it also means Meridian's own performance-management process is not catching the mismatch before the employee quits.
- Spec: retained_6mo (and reopen_rate_6mo_pct, sep_reason indicators) ~ treated_app:C(post_ai) + C(post_ai) + controls + center FE + start-month FE, cluster center
- Sample: 41,677 permanent hires with an observed six-month outcome
- Verdict: supported for retention and reopen rate; the separation-reason split shows voluntary exits rising (statistically distinguishable from zero), not performance/attendance exits (not distinguishable from zero at 95%) -- report this nuance rather than the simpler 'worse hires get fired more' story.

## H2 -- Whitfield's puzzle is the pilot-wave effect  (E04/E05)
- Finding: Pilot-wave centers (2021 vendor pilot -- the largest centers with the strongest IT, i.e. Whitfield's 'best-run' centers) show retention of 0.878 in 2021-22 while on the new ATS, essentially flat, then fall to 0.830 by 2024-25. Never-migrated (legacy) centers move only from 0.889 to 0.857 over the same period. The decline appears within every center-size tercile (E05), so size alone does not explain it; it tracks migration wave times calendar era.
- Spec: Descriptive means by migration wave x start year, and by wave x size tercile
- Sample: 41,677 permanent hires with an observed six-month outcome, by migration wave
- Verdict: supported -- pilot centers did NOT deteriorate immediately upon migrating in 2021 (which would point to the new ATS itself), they deteriorated starting in 2023 (which points to the ATS x AI-era interaction). Whitfield's 'best-run centers are worst' observation is explained by those centers having the longest exposure to the new ATS when AI writing became available, not by anything about how they are run.

## H3 -- pool quality vs essay inflation  (E06/E07/E08/E09)
- Finding: The new-ATS essay score rises continuously from 2023 on (a grid search on the split date finds the largest pre/post gap at 2023-12, 11 months after the configured 2023-01 cutoff -- because the rise is a continuing ramp (18.0 in 2021 to 20.7 in 2025 on the new ATS) rather than a one-month jump, a later split date always shows a larger gap. We keep 2023-01 (ChatGPT's public release) as the substantive cutoff and treat the gradualism itself as a finding: this looks like adoption of a widely available tool, not a single policy change.). Controlling for center and month fixed effects and observable applicant traits, essay scores on the new ATS rise 1.68 points (95% CI [1.23, 2.13]) relative to legacy after the AI era begins. Objective attributes (resume score, license, prior experience, referral, employment status) show no comparable shift (E08), and flex-placement reopen rates -- the pool NOT selected on the essay -- are flat by ATS regime (E09).
- Spec: essay_rubric_score ~ treated_app:C(post_ai) + C(post_ai) + controls + center FE + month FE, cluster center; parallel specs for resume_score and flex reopen_rate_pct
- Sample: All reviewed applications (n≈529k) for essay/resume; all flex placements (n=28,672) for reopen
- Verdict: supported -- the pool did not get objectively better; the essay score is what moved, and it moved only on the system that allows untimed, pasted answers, only after AI writing tools became widely available.

## H4 -- recruiter capacity is binding and worsening  (E10)
- Finding: Applications per opening rose from 150 to 217 (2021 to 2025) while recruiter minutes per application fell from 6.22 to 2.95 and days to fill rose from 22.4 to 29.6. More applications per opening is associated with fewer recruiter minutes per application (-0.0233 minutes per additional applicant, 95% CI [-0.0241, -0.0225], center+month FE) -- recruiters are spreading a fixed weekly capacity across a growing queue. The new ATS itself draws 348 more applications per center-month in the post-AI era (95% CI [99, 596]), plausibly because an untimed, paste-friendly section is easier to complete, compounding the capacity strain on exactly the centers where the essay is least informative.
- Spec: applications_received, recruiter_minutes_per_application, days_to_fill ~ capacity/ATS regressors + center FE + month FE, cluster center
- Sample: 2,400 center-months, 40 centers, 2021-01 to 2025-12
- Verdict: supported -- recruiter capacity has not scaled with volume, and the new ATS adds to that volume; this is a real, independent process failure alongside the essay's loss of signal, and it is part of the 'where the process fails' answer.

## H5 -- which screen components still work  (E11)
- Finding: Controlling for other inputs, the essay's own contribution to six-month retention is 0.0070 under legacy, 0.0105 under the new ATS before the AI era, and -0.0013 under the new ATS after (E11/E11b). License and referral remain positive predictors of retention in every regime (license: {'legacy': 0.0265, 'new_pre': 0.0282, 'new_post': 0.049}; referral: {'legacy': 0.0193, 'new_pre': 0.0169, 'new_post': 0.0324}) -- these are the parts of the process that still work. The essay's weight inside the recruiter's own screen-score formula is essentially unchanged across regimes (0.250 legacy, 0.250 new-pre, 0.249 new-post, all with tight CIs -- E11c): the screen score looks like a fixed formula, not a judgment that has adapted to the essay's declining validity. The interview score is a weak, statistically indistinguishable-from-zero predictor of retention under legacy (0.0032) and new-pre (0.0010), but becomes a strong, significant predictor under new-post (0.0318, 95% CI [0.0241, 0.0396]) -- as the essay stopped screening, the interview appears to be picking up more of the differentiating work, for whatever pool of candidates makes it that far.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ essay + resume + license + prior_years + referral + interview + center FE + start-month FE, run separately per regime; recruiter_screen_score ~ same predictors, per regime; interview-only spec per regime
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome; all reviewed applications for the screen-score regression
- Verdict: mixed -- license and referral hold up across regimes (process gets this right); the essay's mechanical weight in the screen-score formula has not moved despite its predictive content flipping sign, which is itself the process failure; the interview's growing predictive power in new-post is a candidate foundation for a remedy (option c).

## H6 -- does the essay still predict outcomes  (E12/E12b/E12c/E12d)
- Finding: Among permanent hires, controlling for resume, license, prior experience, referral and interview score, the essay-score slope on six-month retention is 0.0074 under legacy (95% CI [0.0059, 0.0089]), 0.0107 under the new ATS pre-2023, and -0.0026 under the new ATS post-2023 (95% CI [-0.0049, -0.0002]) -- the essay's advantage disappears and the point estimate turns negative. The same pattern holds among flex placements, the pool NOT selected on the essay: a higher essay score predicts a 0.1569-point LOWER reopen rate per essay point under legacy (95% CI [-0.1722, -0.1415]) versus -0.0696 under new-post (95% CI [-0.0832, -0.0560]). E12's bar chart shows the same thing nonparametrically: the top essay bin's retention edge over the bottom bin is visible under legacy and new-pre, and gone under new-post.
- Spec: Spec A (hires): retained_6mo ~ essay:C(regime) + C(regime) + controls + center FE + start-month FE, cluster center. Spec B (flex): reopen_rate_pct ~ essay:C(regime) + C(regime) + controls + certification FE + center FE + placement-month FE, cluster center.
- Sample: Spec A: 41,677 hires with an observed six-month outcome. Spec B: 28,672 flex placements, weighted-by-claims robustness in E12d.
- Verdict: supported -- the comparison is the within-regime slope of essay score on the outcome, on two different samples (hires, who ARE selected on the essay, and flex placements, who are NOT). For this to mean 'the essay stopped screening' rather than something else, migration timing must be unrelated to any other driver of retention/reopen changes -- addressed directly in H9.

## H7 -- mechanism: time in the section  (E13/E13b)
- Finding: Among applicants who spent 15+ minutes in the situational section, the essay-score slope on six-month retention is essentially unchanged across regimes: 0.00384 (legacy) vs 0.00329 (new-post), both positive with CIs well clear of zero. Among applicants who spent under 15 minutes, the slope collapses from 0.00223 (legacy) to -0.00052 (new-post, 95% CI [-0.00193, 0.00090], not distinguishable from zero) -- the signal loss is concentrated entirely in fast essays. The essay-score-on-minutes relationship itself reverses sign: +0.0455 (legacy), +0.0339 (new-pre), -0.0211 (new-post) -- under legacy, spending longer on the section predicts a HIGHER score; under the new ATS after 2023, spending LESS time predicts a higher score, the signature of a prepared answer being pasted in quickly.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ essay:C(regime):C(fast15), center+start-month FE, fast15 = essay_section_minutes < 15; essay_rubric_score ~ essay_section_minutes, per regime, center+application-month FE
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome; all reviewed applications for the essay-vs-minutes regression
- Verdict: supported -- time in section is a low-cost signal Meridian already collects and is not currently using; among slow (15+ minute) essays the section still works as well as it ever did, which bounds the size of the fix needed and rules out 'the essay is worthless now' as an overstatement.

## H8 -- placebo: other inputs did not lose power  (E14)
- Finding: Every placebo input gets MORE predictive of retention in new-post relative to legacy, not less: resume score 0.00974 -> 0.02267; adjuster license 0.02947 -> 0.06045; prior claims years 0.00562 -> 0.00785; interview score 0.00317 -> 0.03184. All four are stable-to-rising while the essay's slope on the same outcome falls from +0.0074 to -0.0026 (E12b) and flips sign. This is a stronger placebo result than 'stable': the essay is not just an outlier among noisy measures, it is the one input moving in the opposite direction from every other input in the same regression.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ <var>:C(regime) + C(regime), center+start-month FE, per variable
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome (interview_score restricted to interviewed applicants)
- Verdict: strongly supported -- rules out a general center-wide quality or measurement shift as the explanation; the pattern is specific to the essay, and the other inputs becoming MORE informative is consistent with recruiters and interviewers leaning harder on them as the essay's value eroded (or with a pool that is more heterogeneous on objective traits once the essay stops doing any filtering).

## H9 -- identification, bounds, and the defended range  (E15/E16)
- Finding: Pre-2023, the essay slope on retention is close and not clearly distinguishable between pilot centers (0.00907, se 0.00126, n=8,012) and never-migrated centers (0.00750, se 0.00144, n=2,393) -- pilot centers were not already different before the AI era. The event study (E15) shows a small, roughly stable gap between migrating and never-migrated centers before migration (0.014, never-migrated higher) that widens to 0.043 after migration -- consistent with, not a pre-existing trend that would explain away, the post-migration divergence; the widening (0.029) is in the same ballpark as the H1 difference-in-differences estimate in E01b, a useful cross-check between two different research designs. Local unemployment and wage trends move similarly regardless of ATS (E16). The important caveat: with only 40 center clusters, the wild-cluster bootstrap on the headline essay x new-post interaction gives p=0.49 -- NOT significant at conventional levels, even though the standard cluster-robust confidence interval excludes zero. We report this plainly as a real hole: the point estimate and its direction are corroborated by the flex-placement sample, the event study, and the placebo test (H8), but the formal inference with only 40 clusters is weaker than the cluster-robust CI alone suggests, and a reader should not treat the interaction as 'precisely estimated.' Signal loss estimated on hires (selected on the essay, attenuated, and the slope changes sign so the ratio exceeds 100%) is 135% of the legacy slope; estimated on flex placements (not selected on the essay) is 56%. We report the range [56%, 135%] of legacy predictive power lost, not a point estimate, because the two samples select on the essay differently and bracket the truth from opposite directions.
- Spec: Pre-period subsample regressions; event-study means in quarterly bins around migration date vs a matched pseudo-event date for never-migrated centers; yearly labor-market means by ATS; wild-cluster bootstrap (299 reps) on the H6 headline coefficient
- Sample: Pre-2023 hires in pilot vs never-migrated centers; hires at 2023/2024-wave centers +/-12 months around migration vs never-migrated centers over the same calendar window; center-month panel for labor market
- Verdict: mixed -- the direction and mechanism are well corroborated across four independent checks (pilot-vs-never pre-period parity, event-study timing, flex-sample replication, placebo specificity), but the wild-cluster bootstrap means we cannot claim the headline coefficient is precisely estimated with only 40 center clusters. Name this as the hole. Other holes: we cannot observe whether any individual applicant used AI; we cannot see the ~93% of applications that were never reviewed; migration-wave assignment (IT readiness, center size) could still correlate with something unobserved that also changed around 2023; 2025 cohorts are partly censored.

## H10 -- cost of lost information (worksheet unit test + range)  (E17)
- Finding: Worksheet unit test passes: 2025-average inputs reproduce the prompt's worked example (C_hire~=$18,942, hiring+separation cost per retained employee ~=$26,103, quality cost ~=$11,800, all within 2%). Status quo (new ATS, post-AI-era hires) shows r=0.834, days_to_fill=29.8, reopen=8.59%, cost per retained employee $23,134 excluding quality or $40,022 including it. Against legacy centers in 2024-25 (option-b proxy), the gap is $1,313 excluding quality and $2,506 including it, per retained employee. Against the same new ATS before the AI era (2021-22, costed at 2025 rates), the gap is $1,380 excluding quality and $3,241 including it. We report the range [$2,506, $3,241] per retained employee as the cost of the lost essay signal, because the two counterfactuals bracket it from different angles (a different population of centers vs the same centers at an earlier time) and neither alone is definitive.
- Spec: cost_per_retained = C_hire/r + C_sep*(1/r-1) + [C_quality], per the prompt's worksheet; C_hire/C_sep/C_quality inputs from 2025 center-month averages; r, days_to_fill, reopen from each scenario's hire cohort
- Sample: Status quo n=16,391; legacy-2024-25 n=2,487; pre-AI-new-ATS n=5,920
- Verdict: supported -- unit test passes; whether to monetize quality changes the size of the gap substantially (as the prompt warns it might), so we report both.

## H11 -- cost each option against the status quo  (E19)
- Finding: (a) Do nothing, trended one year on the observed 2024-25 retention slope (+0.0004/yr): r falls to 0.820, cost per retained employee rises to $40,465 (incl. quality), $+443 vs today. (b) Applicants bear it, proxied by the seven never-migrated centers' 2024-25 experience: r=0.857, cost per retained employee $37,516, a saving of $2,506 vs status quo -- BUT legacy centers also receive far fewer applications per opening (apps_received 2539/month vs 4720/month at new-ATS centers in 2024-25) and are smaller/differently located, so this is a proxy, not a clean estimate of what re-timing the section at every center would do. (c) Meridian bears it (structured-interview add-on): under the STATED ASSUMPTION that it recovers half the retention gap to legacy at the cost of 15 extra recruiter minutes per application and 3 extra days to fill, cost per retained employee is $45,795. (d) Bear it after hiring (a $500 six-month retention bonus assumed to close one-third of the gap): cost per retained employee $40,407. Options (c) and (d) are NOT estimated from observed Meridian data -- they require explicit, stated assumptions about how much retention they would buy, because Meridian has not run them; this is named as a limitation, not hidden.
- Spec: Same worksheet as H10; (a) trended on observed slope; (b) legacy-center 2024-25 cohort; (c),(d) counterfactual r assumptions stated explicitly in code and notes
- Sample: See E19 for n by row; (c) and (d) do not have an observed n because they are hypothetical policies
- Verdict: supported as a comparison framework; the ranking between (b), (c) and (d) is sensitive to the stated assumptions for (c) and (d), which is why H12 runs the sensitivity analysis rather than treating this table as final.

## H12 -- sensitivity and what would change the recommendation  (E20)
- Finding: Option (b)'s advantage over the status quo (excluding quality) shrinks as days-to-fill rises; roughly 6 extra vacancy days at the 2025 vacancy cost ($219/day) would erase the saving from improved retention alone, holding r at its observed legacy level. See E20/E20b for the full grid over r and days-to-fill. We would recommend (c) instead of (b) if a timed/no-paste section cuts applicant volume enough to push days-to-fill up by more than roughly this many days, or if applicant-experience costs (fewer/worse applicants applying at all) turn out to be large. We would recommend (d) instead if separations cluster late (months 4-6) rather than early, making a probation-period fix cheap relative to fixing the screen -- check the separation-timing distribution before finalizing -- but E18 shows separations are front-loaded (47% occur in months 1-2, only 33% in months 4-6), which is why option (d) is costed here with a smaller assumed recovery than (b) or (c): a probation review timed around month 3+ arrives after most of the damage is already done. We would recommend doing nothing if the wild-cluster-bootstrap caveat from H9 turns out to reflect a true null effect rather than a small-cluster inference problem -- i.e., if the gap does not replicate in Round 2's additional data.
- Spec: Cost-worksheet formula evaluated over a grid of r (near option-b's observed value) and days-to-fill deltas (0/5/10/15), other inputs at 2025 averages
- Sample: 2,400 center-months for 2025-average rates; grid is a sensitivity exercise, not a new estimation sample
- Verdict: supported as a defensible range and an explicit statement of what would flip the recommendation, per the prompt's requirement.
