# Meridian Casualty: Hiring Is Broken. Can You Fix It?

Econ Week 2026 case competition, Round 1 written analysis. Team: Ethan Friedman, Austin Glick, Danny Shin and Sam Stark. Every number cites an appendix exhibit, which carries sample sizes, standard errors and confidence intervals.

## Recommendation: shift the cost of screening back onto applicants. Re-time the situational section, block pasting, and flag fast answers

Meridian should move the situational section (the essay, in the data) on its new applicant tracking system (ATS) back to the timed, no-paste format its seven legacy centers still use, and show recruiters the minutes each applicant spent in the section. That is option (b). Against the status quo it lowers the cost of one retained employee by **$713 to $1,556** when operational quality is left out, and by **$2,548 to $3,392** when it is monetized (E19). At Meridian's current new-ATS hiring pace that is **$4.9 million to $10.6 million a year** before quality and before the vendor's implementation charge, which the data do not contain (E19). The difference-in-differences gain alone gives the low end, and the seven legacy centers' current results give the high end (E19 rows b-low to b-high). The quality figures also rest on the legacy centers' lower reopen rate, whose difference-in-differences interaction on its own does not clear zero (E02b), so we defend the excluding-quality range.

Since 2023 the essay no longer predicts who stays on the new system, it still carries part of that signal on the legacy system, and the legacy centers retain more hires today (E12, E12b, E12d, E01b, E17). Only option (b) has a retention effect we can observe in Meridian's own centers rather than assume.

Seven small legacy centers carry most of the weight here, and that is the hole in our analysis. They sit in the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic, and their own essay signal is eroding. Matching them to new-ATS centers on size and region does not shrink their advantage, but seven centers can only bound it (E15f, E07b, E12e). Their result therefore caps what (b) can deliver, and we report the range rather than its midpoint.

## (a) The process still measures the right things, but it keeps full weight on an essay signal that has vanished, and recruiters have half the time to read it

**What works.** Four screen inputs still predict who lasts six months, and each predicts better in the AI era than before it. Only the interview on the legacy system before 2023 has a slope not distinguishable from zero (E11b). Holding the other inputs fixed, an adjuster license raises six-month retention by 1.6 points on the legacy system before 2023 and 4.9 points on the new system after 2023. A referral goes from 1.6 to 3.2 points, the resume score from 1.0 to 1.9 points per unit, and the structured interview from 0.9 to 2.7 points per unit (E11, E11b, E14). Applicant volume works. Openings fill in about a month and each draws over a hundred applications (E10, E10b).

**First failure: the screen has not adapted.** The recruiter screen score is a fixed formula. The essay's weight inside it is 0.250 in every ATS-and-era cell, with an R-squared of 0.82 to 0.86, exactly what it was when the essay worked (E11c). Meanwhile the essay's own predictive slope on retention has gone from 0.8 points per rubric point on the legacy system before 2023 to zero on the new system after 2023 (E11b). Meridian is still paying a quarter of its screen for information that is no longer there.

**Second failure: recruiters get less time per application every year, and the new-ATS centers get the least.** Applications per opening rose from 150 in 2021 to 217 in 2025 while recruiter minutes per application fell from 6.2 to 3.0 and days to fill rose from 22 to 30 (all centers pooled, E10e, and by ATS, E10b). Each extra applicant per opening costs 0.023 minutes of review per application (E10f). More applicants and fewer minutes per application both lengthen the time to fill (E10c). The new ATS itself draws 348 more applications per center-month than the legacy system in the AI era, most likely because an untimed section that accepts pasted text is easier to finish (E10d). So the new-ATS centers get both the least informative essays and the least time to read them.

**Whitfield sees the consequence.** Six-month retention on the new ATS fell 2.3 points more than on the legacy system once the AI era began (difference-in-differences −0.023, 95% CI −0.042 to −0.004, 41,677 hires, E01, E01b). Reopen rates on the new ATS are 0.14 to 0.25 percentage points higher in the AI era. The interaction term gives 0.14 and the raw AI-era gap 0.25, and only the 0.25 clears zero (E02, E02b). Separations rose across voluntary, performance and attendance exits, with no single reason carrying the increase (E03, E03b, E03c). That fits a screen that admits people who neither stick nor perform. A labor-market story would show up mainly as voluntary exits. Her "best-run centers are worst" describes the pilot wave. The largest centers migrated first in 2021, held retention at 0.878 through 2022, then fell to 0.830 by 2024-25, while never-migrated centers moved from 0.889 to 0.857. The decline appears in every size tercile (E04, E05).

## (b) The situational section no longer screens on the new system, and we know because its slope on outcomes collapsed in one cell and nowhere else

**The comparison.** We estimate the slope of the essay rubric score on the six-month outcome within each of four cells: legacy or new ATS at the application month, crossed with application before or after January 2023. **On whom.** Two samples. Permanent hires (41,677 with an observed outcome, after dropping 4,589 censored hires, E00) were selected on the essay, so every slope in that sample is attenuated. Flex placements (28,672) applied to Meridian but were placed by the staffing partner on certification, geography and availability. The essay played no part, so their slope comes out cleaner. Both use center and month fixed effects with standard errors clustered by center.

**The result.** Among hires, a rubric point raised retention by 0.87 points on the legacy system before 2023 (95% CI 0.69 to 1.05) and 1.07 points on the new system before 2023 (0.74 to 1.40). On the new system after 2023 the slope is −0.26 points (−0.49 to −0.02), so a higher essay score now predicts slightly worse retention (E12b), and the same reversal appears on hires' reopen rates (E12c). On the legacy system after 2023 it is 0.48 points (0.14 to 0.82), roughly half its old value (E12b). Flex placements tell the same story on reopen rates. Each rubric point lowered the reopen rate by 0.18 points on legacy and 0.21 on the new system before 2023, by 0.12 on legacy after 2023, and by only 0.07 on the new system after 2023 (E12d). E12 shows the same thing without a regression. A 24-30 essay retains better than an 18-23 essay in three cells and worse in the fourth, 0.829 against 0.853. Combining the two samples, the new ATS in the AI era keeps **0% to 39%** of the essay's pre-2023 predictive power and the legacy ATS keeps **55% to 68%** (E15e). The two samples bracket the truth, because hires understate the loss and flex placements do not.

**Time spent in the section explains the collapse.** Among hires who spent 15 minutes or more writing, the essay still predicts retention in every cell, including the new system in the AI era (0.41 points per rubric point, 0.20 to 0.61). Among those who spent under 15 minutes on the new system after 2023, a higher score predicts worse retention (−1.64 points, −2.06 to −1.22) (E07, E13, E13b). Effort used to earn points. On the legacy system before 2023 a minute in the section added 0.054 rubric points. On the new system after 2023 a minute subtracts 0.021 (E13c). A high score written in a few minutes looks like a prepared answer pasted into the box. Meridian already records the minutes and does not use them.

**Placebo.** If something center-wide had changed, every input would have lost power. None did. The resume slope went from 0.9 to 2.3 points, license from 2.0 to 6.1, prior claims years from 0.4 to 0.8, interview from −0.5 to 3.2, over the same cells in which the essay went from 0.87 to −0.26 (E14).

**Timing.** Essay scores rise from 2023 in every new-ATS center regardless of when it migrated, including the 2021 pilots, so the break comes from the AI era meeting an untimed field rather than from the software itself (E06). A grid search over break dates finds a ramp rather than a jump, so any cutoff in 2023 tells the same story (E06b). With center, month and applicant controls, the new ATS raised scores by 0.89 rubric points before 2023 and a further 0.79 (0.58 to 1.00) once AI writing arrived (E06c), while objective attributes drifted slightly down (license −2.6 points, prior claims experience −2.4, referral −1.1, E08, E08b) and the flex pool's reopen rate did not move (E09, E09b), so the scores rose while the pool did not improve.

**Identification.** Before 2023 the pilot centers' essay slope matched the never-migrated centers' (0.010 against 0.008 per rubric point, E15g), so migration did not select on essay validity. The event study shows no pre-trend, then a 0.029 widening after migration, which matches the difference-in-differences estimate from a different design (E15, E15b). Unemployment and wages move together across ATS groups (E16, E16b). The essay's standard deviation among hires is 2.85 to 2.98 in every cell, so the zero slope is not range restriction. The share of high scorers changed instead. In the AI era 23% of new-ATS applicants score 24-30, against 8% of legacy applicants before 2023 (E15c). With 40 centers, a wild-cluster bootstrap on the slope difference between the new-ATS AI-era cell and the legacy pre-2023 cell gives p below 0.01 in both samples (E15d).

**What must be true** for this to mean the essay stopped screening. The staffing partner does not place on the essay (stated in the case). Nothing unobserved changed at migrating centers in 2023 that also changed essay validity (E15 and the pre-period parity above). And the 2023 cutoff is substantively right (E06b). **What we could not determine.** Whether any individual used AI. What the 93% to 95% of applications never reviewed look like (E10e). And how far the legacy system's own erosion continues, since its sub-10-minute share doubled from 6% to 12% and its bootstrap p on erosion is 0.08 for hires and 0.003 for flex (E07b, E15d), so a timed field slows the leak without stopping it.

## (c) Only option (b) has an observed retention effect, and it wins under every bound

We implement Meridian's worksheet exactly and reproduce the worked example (E17a). We cost the status quo on new-ATS hires starting in 2024-25: r = 0.820, 28.9 days to fill, an 8.9% reopen rate, and a cost per retained employee of $23,377 excluding quality and $40,908 including it (E17). Three counterfactuals in which the essay still screened put the cost of the lost information at $713 to $1,830 per retained employee excluding quality and $2,548 to $4,334 including it (E17). We show both because the quality term roughly doubles every gap and can change the ranking.

E19 costs the four options against the status quo per retained employee. **(a) Do nothing** gains nothing. Retention did not move from 2024 to 2025 (+0.000), so extending that trend shifts the cost by $23, which is noise. **(b) Applicants bear it** saves $713 to $1,556 excluding quality, from the difference-in-differences gain alone up to the legacy centers' 2024-25 retention, reopen rate and shorter fill time. The size-and-region matched estimate, $1,379, sits inside that range (E15f, E19). **(c) Meridian bears it**, a structured interview at 15 extra recruiter minutes per reviewed application ($204 per hire) and 3 more days to fill, costs $448 more excluding quality and $469 less including it, if it recovers half the gap. We assume that recovery, since no center has run the longer interview. **(d) Bear it after hiring**, a $500 six-month bonus, costs $305 more either way, because separations are front-loaded. Of those who leave, 47% are gone by month two and only 33% leave in months four to six, before a probation review can act (E18, E18b). Recruiter screening, which the prompt puts inside C_hire, is about $740 per hire (E17b). We leave it out of every scenario. It would fall under (b), which draws fewer applications per opening (E10b, E10d), so leaving it out understates (b)'s saving. Option (c) is charged its extra minutes.

**Sensitivity.** The prompt's warning holds. Option (b) trades fewer separations for possibly more vacancy days. At $219 per vacancy day, (b)'s saving is erased by 2.7 extra days to fill if it only buys the difference-in-differences gain, 4.4 days at the legacy-observed retention, 5.5 at the matched estimate, and 7.4 at the pre-AI level. Including quality, the break-evens are 9.8 to 14.8 days (E20, E20b, E20c). In the data the legacy centers fill faster (27.3 against 28.9 days in the 2024-25 cohorts, E17), and the matched gap is still about 0.7 days in their favor (E15f). The "fewer applicants, longer fill" fear does not show up, though those centers are small.

**What (b) costs applicants.** A timed, no-paste section is harder to complete, and Whitfield is right about that. Start with drop-off. The legacy centers drew 183 applications per opening against 224 on the new system in 2025 (E10b), so a timed field could cost about a fifth of the queue. That is already priced in. The retention, reopen and fill figures (b) borrows from the legacy centers (E19) are what they achieve with the smaller queue, and sourcing cost is charged per opening rather than per applicant (E17a). Then fairness. A timer penalizes slow writers, including applicants who need accommodation, so the vendor ask should include extended time on request and the pilot should report completion rates by applicant group. We could not measure either cost, and drop-off under a timed section is the fifth thing we watch for in the Round 2 packet.

**We would recommend something else if:**

- **(c) instead**, if a timed section pushed days to fill up by more than the break-evens above, or if a pilot showed a structured interview recovers more than 89% of the legacy retention gap at $204 per hire (E20c).
- **(d) instead**, if separations were concentrated in months four to six rather than the first two (E18).
- **Do nothing**, if the Round 2 packet showed the legacy centers' advantage disappears once matched on size and region (on our data it does not, E15f), or that the vendor cannot re-time the section for less than the annual saving in E19.

**Implementation, in three steps.** First, at no cost, show recruiters the minutes-in-section field Meridian already collects and flag answers under 15 minutes (E13b). Second, ask the vendor for a fixed quote to make the section timed and paste-disabled on the new ATS. The annual saving in E19 is the most that change can cost and still pay. Third, pilot the timed section for one hiring quarter in the centers that migrated in 2024, with two stop rules: days to fill rising by more than the break-even in E20c, or applications per opening falling below the legacy centers' level (E10b). The nine 2024-wave centers make about 425 hires a quarter (E00), enough to detect a five-point retention gain but not the 2.3-point lower bound, so use the first quarter to check the stop rules and read retention against the difference-in-differences bound over two to three quarters before rolling out. Five things in the Round 2 packet could change this call: the vendor's cost and feasibility, the matched comparison, a labor-market shock, a revised reopened-claim cost, and applicant drop-off. We have written down what each would do to it.
