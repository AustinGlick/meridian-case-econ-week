
## H0 -- data integrity  (E00)
- Finding: Joins are 1:1 and match the raw files. 4589 of 46266 hires (9.9%) are right-censored and excluded, leaving 41677 hires with an observed six-month outcome. 530 hires have a different ATS at start than at application; we use the application-month ATS throughout because that is when the essay was written.
- Spec: n/a -- data validation
- Sample: All raw files; hires_obs = hires with retained_6mo not null
- Verdict: supported -- starter.load() joins are correct; 7 centers never migrated (matches DATA-DICTIONARY.md and prompt.pdf, which say 7).

## H1 -- retention decline concentrated in new-ATS, post-AI hires  (E01/E02/E03)
- Finding: Six-month retention on the new ATS falls 2.3% more than on legacy once the AI era begins (DiD interaction -0.0232, 95% CI [-0.0423, -0.0041], n=41,677, controls and center/start-month FE, cluster-robust by center). Before 2023 the new ATS had no retention effect (+0.0025, 95% CI [-0.0145, 0.0196]), so the AI-era gap between new and legacy hires is -0.0207 [-0.0376, -0.0037]. Reopen rates: the AI-era gap is +0.25 points [0.07, 0.42], but the DiD interaction itself is +0.14 [-0.03, 0.32] -- not distinguishable from zero, because reopen rates were already slightly higher on the new ATS before 2023. We report the reopen effect as a range [0.14, 0.25] points. By separation reason (DiD interaction): voluntary: +0.0098 [-0.0004, +0.0200] (not distinguishable from zero); performance: +0.0101 [-0.0007, +0.0208] (not distinguishable from zero); attendance: +0.0033 [-0.0052, +0.0118] (not distinguishable from zero). The largest reason-specific DiD is performance and the smallest is attendance; 0 of the three reason-specific interactions clear zero at 95%, so the extra separations are spread across reasons rather than concentrated in one -- consistent with a screen that admits people who neither stick nor perform, not with a labor-market story (which would load on voluntary exits alone).
- Spec: retained_6mo (and reopen_rate_6mo_pct, sep_reason indicators) ~ treated_app + treated_app:post_ai + post_ai + controls + center FE + start-month FE, cluster center
- Sample: 41,677 permanent hires with an observed six-month outcome
- Verdict: supported for retention; mixed for reopen rate (AI-era gap clear, DiD interaction not); the separation-reason split is spread across voluntary and performance exits (0 of 3 reason-specific DiDs clear zero at 95%) -- report the pooled retention DiD as the headline and the reason split as supporting detail, not the reverse.

## H2 -- Whitfield's puzzle is the pilot-wave effect  (E04/E05)
- Finding: Pilot-wave centers (2021 vendor pilot -- the largest centers with the strongest IT, i.e. Whitfield's 'best-run' centers) show retention of 0.878 in 2021-22 while on the new ATS, essentially flat, then fall to 0.830 by 2024-25. Never-migrated (legacy) centers move only from 0.889 to 0.857 over the same period. The decline appears within every center-size tercile (E05), so size alone does not explain it; it tracks migration wave times calendar era.
- Spec: Descriptive means by migration wave x start year, and by wave x size tercile
- Sample: 41,677 permanent hires with an observed six-month outcome, by migration wave
- Verdict: supported -- pilot centers did NOT deteriorate immediately upon migrating in 2021 (which would point to the new ATS itself), they deteriorated starting in 2023 (which points to the ATS x AI-era interaction). Whitfield's 'best-run centers are worst' observation is explained by those centers having the longest exposure to the new ATS when AI writing became available, not by anything about how they are run.

## H3 -- pool quality vs essay inflation  (E06/E07/E08/E09)
- Finding: The new-ATS essay score rises continuously from 2023 on (a grid search on the split date finds the largest pre/post gap at 2023-12, 11 months after the configured 2023-01 cutoff -- because the rise is a continuing ramp (18.0 in 2021 to 20.7 in 2025 on the new ATS, E07b) rather than a one-month jump, a later split date always shows a larger gap. We keep 2023-01 (ChatGPT's public release) as the substantive cutoff and treat the gradualism itself as a finding: this looks like adoption of a widely available tool, not a single policy change.). Controlling for center and month fixed effects and observable applicant traits, the new ATS raised essay scores by 0.89 points even before the AI era (untimed answers are longer and more polished), and by a further 0.79 points (95% CI [0.58, 1.00], the DiD interaction) once AI writing became available -- an AI-era gap of 1.68 points [1.46, 1.90] over legacy (E06c). Objective attributes move the OTHER way: the resume-score DiD is -0.0100 standard deviations and license, prior claims, referral and employment shares all fall by 1 to 3 points on the new ATS in the AI era (E08/E08b), so the pool did not get better while scores rose; flex-placement reopen rates -- the pool NOT selected on the essay -- do not move by ATS regime (E09/E09b: DiD -0.075 points, 95% CI [-0.342, 0.191]). The legacy system is not immune: its mean score drifts from 17.2 to 18.1 and its share of sub-10-minute essays from 6.1% to 11.7% (E07b), versus 5.9% to 28.3% on the new ATS -- applicants can draft elsewhere and type it in; a timed field slows the leak, it does not stop it.
- Spec: essay_rubric_score ~ treated_app + treated_app:post_ai + controls + center FE + month FE (post_ai absorbed), cluster center; parallel specs for each objective attribute and for flex reopen_rate_pct
- Sample: All reviewed applications (n≈529k) for essay/resume; all flex placements (n=28,672) for reopen
- Verdict: supported -- the pool did not get objectively better; the essay score is what moved, and it moved only on the system that allows untimed, pasted answers, only after AI writing tools became widely available.

## H4 -- recruiter capacity is binding and worsening  (E10)
- Finding: Applications per opening rose from 150 to 217 (2021 to 2025) while recruiter minutes per application fell from 6.22 to 2.95 and days to fill rose from 22.4 to 29.6 (all centers pooled, E10e; by ATS, E10b). More applications per opening is associated with fewer recruiter minutes per application (-0.0233 minutes per additional applicant, 95% CI [-0.0241, -0.0225], center+month FE, E10f) -- recruiters are spreading a fixed weekly capacity across a growing queue. The new ATS itself draws 348 more applications per center-month than legacy in the AI era (95% CI [99, 596], E10d; the pre-2023 new-ATS effect is negative and the DiD interaction larger, so the AI-era gap is the defensible number), plausibly because an untimed, paste-friendly section is easier to complete, compounding the capacity strain on exactly the centers where the essay is least informative.
- Spec: applications_received ~ treated_app + treated_app:post_ai + center FE + month FE; recruiter_minutes_per_application, days_to_fill ~ capacity regressors + center FE + month FE; cluster center
- Sample: 2,400 center-months, 40 centers, 2021-01 to 2025-12
- Verdict: supported -- recruiter capacity has not scaled with volume, and the new ATS adds to that volume; this is a real, independent process failure alongside the essay's loss of signal, and it is part of the 'where the process fails' answer.

## H5 -- which screen components still work  (E11/E11b/E11c)
- Finding: Controlling for the other inputs (E11b, n by cell: {'legacy_post': 6326, 'legacy_pre': 13040, 'new_post': 16391, 'new_pre': 5920}), the essay's slope on six-month retention is 0.0081 [0.0062, 0.0101] per point on the legacy ATS before 2023, 0.0049 [0.0015, 0.0083] on legacy after 2023, 0.0105 [0.0069, 0.0140] on the new ATS before 2023, and -0.0013 [-0.0037, 0.0010] on the new ATS after 2023. License (0.0163 [0.0054, 0.0272] -> 0.0490 [0.0386, 0.0593]), referral (0.0156 [0.0050, 0.0263] -> 0.0324 [0.0212, 0.0436]), resume score (0.0100 [0.0047, 0.0152] -> 0.0192 [0.0135, 0.0248]) and the interview (0.0086 [-0.0001, 0.0172] -> 0.0268 [0.0188, 0.0348]) all keep or gain predictive power -- these are the parts of the process that still work. The essay's weight inside the recruiter screen score is 0.250 in every cell (E11c; R-squared 0.821-0.862): the screen score is a fixed formula, so the process still weights the essay exactly as it did when the essay worked.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ essay + resume + license + prior_years + referral + interview + center FE + start-month FE, separately per ATS x era cell, cluster center; recruiter_screen_score ~ same inputs, per cell
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome; all reviewed applications for the screen-score regression
- Verdict: mixed -- license, referral, resume and interview hold up or strengthen (the process gets these right); the essay's mechanical weight in the screen formula has not moved while its predictive content went to zero on the new ATS and roughly halved on the legacy ATS -- that unadapted formula is the failure.

## H6 -- does the essay still predict outcomes  (E12/E12b/E12c/E12d/E12e)
- Finding: Comparison: the slope of essay score on the outcome within each ATS x era cell. On whom: (i) permanent hires (selected on the essay, so every slope is attenuated) and (ii) flex placements (not selected on the essay by Meridian). HIRES, retention per essay point (E12b, n=41,677): legacy pre-2023 0.0087 [0.0069, 0.0105]; legacy post-2023 0.0048 [0.0014, 0.0082]; new pre-2023 0.0107 [0.0074, 0.0140]; new post-2023 -0.0026 [-0.0049, -0.0002]. HIRES, reopen rate (E12c): legacy pre -0.147 -> legacy post -0.093; new pre -0.180 -> new post 0.035 (sign flips). FLEX, reopen rate (E12d, n=28,672): legacy pre -0.179 [-0.1986, -0.1602]; legacy post -0.122 [-0.1461, -0.0981]; new pre -0.207 [-0.2443, -0.1694]; new post -0.069 [-0.0831, -0.0559]. So: on the new ATS in the AI era the essay carries no usable signal for hires and about a third of its former signal for flex; on the legacy ATS in the AI era it keeps roughly half (hires) to two-thirds (flex). Spec C (E12e) shows the same within centers that migrated in 2023/24 (0.0063 before -> -0.0016 after), but never-migrated centers over the same calendar window also fall (0.0067 -> -0.0032, n=2,359), which is why the four-cell comparison, not migration alone, is the identifying comparison. E12 shows it nonparametrically: the 24-30 bin out-retains the 18-23 bin in every cell except new ATS post-2023, where it under-retains it.
- Spec: Spec A (hires): retained_6mo ~ essay:C(regime4) + C(regime4) + controls + center FE + start-month FE, cluster center. Spec B (flex): reopen_rate_pct ~ essay:C(regime4) + C(regime4) + controls + certification FE + center FE + placement-month FE, cluster center (claims-weighted robustness in E12d). Spec C: essay:C(post) within +/-12 months of migration.
- Sample: Spec A: 41,677 hires with an observed six-month outcome. Spec B: 28,672 flex placements. Spec C: 8,327 + 2,359 hires.
- Verdict: supported -- the essay no longer works as a screen on the new ATS in the AI era and works at reduced strength on the legacy ATS. What must be true: selection into the hire sample on the essay must not have changed in a way that produces a zero slope by itself (the essay SD among hires is 2.85-2.98 across cells, E15c), and flex placement must not be selected on the essay (stated in the case). Identification threats are in H9.

## H7 -- mechanism: time in the section  (E13/E13b/E13c)
- Finding: Among hires who spent 15+ minutes in the section, the essay slope on retention is 0.0075 (legacy pre), 0.0040 (legacy post), 0.0090 (new pre) and 0.0041 (new post, 95% CI [0.0020, 0.0061]) -- still positive everywhere. Among hires who spent under 15 minutes it is 0.0080 (legacy pre), -0.0007 (legacy post), 0.0124 (new pre) and -0.0164 (new post, 95% CI [-0.0206, -0.0122]): a high score produced fast on the new ATS in the AI era predicts WORSE retention. The score-per-minute relationship (E13c) is +0.0543 (legacy pre), +0.0299 (legacy post), +0.0339 (new pre), -0.0211 (new post): effort used to earn points; on the new ATS after 2023, less time predicts a higher score, the signature of a prepared answer pasted in.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ essay:C(regime4):C(fast15) + C(regime4)*C(fast15) + center + start-month FE, cluster center; essay_rubric_score ~ minutes per cell with center + application-month FE
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome; all reviewed applications for E13c
- Verdict: supported -- time in section is a free signal Meridian already collects and does not use; slow essays still work, fast high-scoring ones are now a negative signal.

## H8 -- placebo: other inputs did not lose power  (E14)
- Finding: Univariate slopes on retention, legacy pre-2023 -> new post-2023: resume score 0.0089 -> 0.0227; license 0.0196 -> 0.0605; prior claims years 0.0041 -> 0.0079; interview -0.0048 -> 0.0318. Every placebo input holds or strengthens while the essay's slope on the same outcome goes from 0.0087 to -0.0026 (E12b). The essay is the only input moving the wrong way.
- Spec: retained_6mo ~ <var>:C(regime4) + C(regime4), center + start-month FE, per variable
- Sample: 41,677 hires with an observed outcome (interview_score: interviewed only)
- Verdict: supported -- rules out a center-wide quality or measurement shift; the loss is specific to the essay. Other inputs strengthening is consistent with the essay no longer filtering, leaving more true-quality variation for the other inputs to explain.

## H9 -- identification, bounds, and the defended range  (E15/E15b/E15c/E15d/E15e/E15f/E15g/E16)
- Finding: (1) Pre-2023 the essay slope is the same in pilot centers (0.0098, se 0.0012, n=8,012) and never-migrated centers (0.0083, se 0.0019, n=2,393): pilot centers were not different before the AI era. (2) Event study (E15/E15b): the never-minus-migrating retention gap is 0.014 in the 12 months before migration and 0.043 after; the widening (0.029) matches the H1 DiD (E01b) from a different design. (3) Unemployment and wages move together by ATS (E16). (4) Selection (E15c): the essay SD among hires is 2.85-2.98 in every cell, so the zero slope in new_post is not range restriction; what changed is that 23% of new-ATS AI-era applicants score 24-30 versus 8% of legacy pre-2023 applicants. (5) Small clusters (E15d): the wild-cluster bootstrap p on the new_post-minus-legacy_pre slope difference is 0.000 (hires) and 0.000 (flex); on the legacy_post-minus-legacy_pre difference it is 0.080 (hires) and 0.003 (flex). Defended range (E15e): the new ATS in the AI era retains [0%, 39%] of the essay's pre-2023 predictive power; the legacy ATS in the AI era retains [55%, 68%]. It is a range because hires are selected on the essay (attenuated) and flex placements are not; the truth sits between them. (6) Matched comparison of the legacy centers (E15f), the option-(b) proxy: in the 2024-25 window the seven never-migrated centers' six-month retention gap over new-ATS hires is 0.036 [-0.003, 0.075] unmatched (33 comparison centers, 11,440 hires vs 1,737 legacy hires); restricted to the same regions and size band it is 0.050 [0.005, 0.096] (9 centers, 2,050 hires; wild-cluster bootstrap p 0.074 with 16 clusters); nearest-neighbour k=1 0.034 [-0.008, 0.075] (5 centers); k=2 0.046 [0.001, 0.091] (7 centers); propensity-reweighted 0.057 [0.002, 0.113] (19 centers). Reopen gap (points): -0.88 unmatched, -0.88 to -0.47 matched; days-to-fill gap: -1.16 days unmatched, -0.78 to -0.71 matched (legacy centers still fill faster). The matched retention gaps (0.034 to 0.057) all sit at or above the H1 DiD bound of 0.023 (E01b), so matching does not move the legacy advantage toward the DiD bound; but with 7 legacy centers against 5-19 comparison centers the CIs span roughly 0 to 0.1 and this is a bound, not a clean estimate.
- Spec: Pre-period subsample regressions; event-study means; yearly labor-market means; selection table; wild-cluster bootstrap (Rademacher, 299 reps) on explicit slope-difference columns with the restricted model imposing H0; E15f: outcome ~ legacy + start-month (or month) FE on the 2024-25 window, comparison centers restricted (region + size band) or reweighted (nearest-neighbour k=1/2 within region on size_index; propensity p/(1-p)), cluster center, 12-26 clusters
- Sample: Pre-2023 hires in pilot vs never-migrated centers; hires +/-12 months around migration; 2,400 center-months; all applications; hires and flex for the bootstrap; E15f: 1,737 legacy and 11,440 new-ATS hires starting 2024-01 or later, plus 2024-25 center-months
- Verdict: supported -- direction, timing, placebo and both samples agree, and the headline slope differences survive small-cluster inference. Named holes: we cannot observe AI use; we cannot see the ~94% of applications never reviewed; migration waves were chosen on size and IT readiness, so something unobserved that changed at large centers around 2023 could still contribute; the legacy ATS is itself eroding, so the 7 legacy centers are an imperfect proxy for a timed section; 2025 cohorts are partly censored. E15f verdict: matching the legacy centers to new-ATS centers on size and region does NOT shrink their 2024-25 retention advantage toward the H1 DiD bound (0.023) -- the matched gap is 0.034 to 0.057 against 0.036 unmatched, all at or above the bound -- so the (b) range in E19 keeps the DiD bound as its low end and the legacy-observed gap as its high end; with 7 legacy centers this is a bound on the proxy's bias, not a clean estimate, and the Round 2 trigger (advantage disappears once matched) has not fired on this data.

## H10 -- cost of lost information (worksheet unit test + range)  (E17/E17a/E17b)
- Finding: Worksheet unit test passes (C_hire $18,942; hiring+separation $26,103; quality $11,800 -- all within 2% of the prompt). Status quo (new-ATS hires starting 2024-25, n=11,440): r=0.819, days to fill 28.9, reopen 8.92%, cost per retained employee $23,377 excluding operational quality, $40,908 including it. Against three counterfactuals in which the essay still screened -- A: legacy centers 2024-25 (r=0.857); A': status quo plus the H1 DiD gain of 0.023 (r=0.843); B: the same centers before AI (r=0.882) -- the cost of the lost signal per retained employee is $713 to $1,830 excluding quality and $2,548 to $4,334 including it (E17). At 6,830 retained seats a year on the new ATS (8,334 hires x r), that is $4.9M-$12.5M a year excluding quality, $17.4M-$29.6M including it. Recruiter screening time adds about $740 per hire (4% of C_hire) and is excluded from the worksheet figures (E17b).
- Spec: cost_per_retained = C_hire/r + C_sep*(1/r-1) + [C_quality]; inputs = 2025 center-month averages; r and reopen from each scenario's observed cohort (A' uses the E01b DiD)
- Sample: Status quo n=11,440; A n=2,487; B n=5,920
- Verdict: supported -- unit test passes; the range is a range because the counterfactuals differ in population (A), design (A') and period (B), and because monetising quality roughly doubles the gap, which the prompt warns can change the ranking.

## H11 -- cost each option against the status quo  (E19)
- Finding: Per retained employee, versus the status quo ($23,377 excl. quality / $40,908 incl.): (a) do nothing $-23 if the 2024-25 retention change (+0.000) continues; (b) applicants bear it $-1,130 excl. quality / $-2,966 incl. at status-quo days to fill, with a range of $-713 to $-1,556 excl. quality ($-2,548 to $-3,392 incl.) across the DiD-only, legacy-observed and size-and-region matched bounds (the matched row, E15f, gives r=0.866 and $-1,379 excl. / $-3,116 incl., so matching does not pull (b) toward the DiD bound); (c) Meridian bears it $+448 / $-469 under the stated assumptions (half the gap recovered, $204 of extra recruiter time per hire, +3 days); (d) bear it after hiring $+305 / $+305 for a $500 bonus recovering a sixth of the gap. Annual, at 6,830 retained seats: (b) saves $4.9M-$10.6M excl. quality ($17.4M-$23.2M incl.; low bound = DiD-only gain, high bound = legacy-observed r and days to fill), before any implementation cost, which the data do not contain. Caveats: the legacy centers are smaller, receive fewer applications, and their own essay signal is eroding (E12b/E12d), so (b) as 'copy the legacy configuration' is an upper bound on a timed section alone; (c) and (d) are assumption-driven.
- Spec: Same worksheet as H10; (a') trended; (b) legacy 2024-25 cohort at status-quo days to fill, bounded by the DiD-only gain, the legacy-observed days to fill and the size-and-region matched gap (E15f, shared helper utils.matched_legacy_gaps); (c),(d) assumed recoveries stated in the row labels
- Sample: See E19 n column; (c) and (d) have no observed n
- Verdict: supported as a comparison framework -- (b) is the only option whose retention effect is observed rather than assumed, and it is cheaper than the status quo under every bound; (c) beats the status quo only if it recovers most of the gap at low extra recruiter time (E20); (d) roughly breaks even.

## H12 -- sensitivity and what would change the recommendation  (E20/E20b/E20c)
- Finding: Option (b)'s saving per retained employee is erased by 3 extra vacancy days if it only buys the DiD retention gain (r=0.843), 4 days at the legacy-observed r=0.857, 5 days at the size-and-region matched r=0.866 (E15f), and 7 days at the pre-AI r=0.882 (excluding quality; including quality the break-evens are 10, 12, 13 and 15 days). Vacancy cost is $219/day. Legacy centers today fill faster, not slower, than new-ATS centers (27.3 vs 28.9 days, E10b), so the 'fewer applicants means longer to fill' fear is not visible in the data, but those centers are small. Option (c) at +15 recruiter minutes per reviewed application ($204/hire) and +3 days must recover at least 89% of the legacy retention gap to beat the status quo excluding quality. We would recommend (c) instead of (b) if a timed section pushed days to fill up by more than the break-even above, or if a structured interview were shown (pilot) to recover more than 89% of the gap at that cost. We would recommend (d) instead if separations were concentrated in months 4-6, but 33% of them are (E18) and 47% happen in months 1-2, before a probation review could act. We would recommend doing nothing if the Round 2 packet showed the legacy centers' retention advantage disappears once matched on size and region (on our data it does not: matched gap 0.046 vs unmatched 0.037, E15f, but with 7 legacy centers that is a bound), or that the vendor cannot re-time the section at a cost below roughly the annual saving in E19.
- Spec: Worksheet over a grid of r and extra days to fill; break-even days = saving / (vacancy_cost_per_day / r); option (c) break-even recovery by grid search
- Sample: 2,400 center-months for 2025 rates; sensitivity exercise, not a new estimation sample
- Verdict: supported -- the recommendation for (b) survives every r bound at zero extra days and survives the legacy-observed r up to about a week of extra vacancy per hire; the three 'recommend something else if' triggers are stated.

## H13 -- reweighting the screen away from the essay, judged among hires  (E21)
- Finding: Among the 16,391 new-ATS AI-era hires, the top half within center under the current formula retains 0.826; under the same formula with the essay weight set to zero the top half retains 0.867. The 3,699 hires the essay-free ranking moves INTO the top half retain 0.864 (reopen 8.07%) against 0.772 (reopen 9.55%) for the ones it moves OUT. Doubling the license and referral weights on top changes little (0.869). This is a lower-bound check: outcomes exist only for people the current formula selected, so the full effect of reweighting on who gets hired cannot be estimated from this data, and it is not costed in E19.
- Spec: recruiter_screen_score ~ six screen inputs + center FE + application-month FE on new_post applications gives the weights; hires re-ranked within center by the fitted score with the essay weight zeroed (and, separately, license/referral doubled); mean outcomes by group
- Sample: 16,391 new-ATS hires applying 2023-01 or later with an observed outcome; 255,535 reviewed applications for the weights
- Verdict: supported as a free first step -- among people already hired, dropping the essay from the ranking favors hires who retain about 9 points better; it does not replace option (b), which restores the essay's information rather than discarding it, and its effect on applicants never hired is unobservable.
